## What to write

The person's main initiatives in the period, each with a line on the person's impact in it and a paragraph on what the person did in it. An initiative is yours to name and to draw together, such as an epic, the pull requests that served it and the plan behind it, or a theme several epics share; give it a short title. Support is work too: answering and fixing in support channels, following delivery through and making sure a rollout goes well. It belongs with the initiative it served, its delivery and its support in one paragraph, even when the support came after the delivery; it makes an initiative of its own only when it served none of the others. Leave out work too small to matter in a review, such as a one-off fix, unless it had a clear impact.

## How to write an initiative

- The impact line says the person's impact: what changed because of their part in the initiative, and for whom, such as who it served or what it unblocked. Start from the initiative's impact, as the sources state it, and narrow it to what the person's part brought about, never the whole initiative's impact for a part of it: one line, so the manager sees it before the detail.
- The paragraph says what the person did, their own part in it, such as leading the design, building the core or taking over from someone, bearing the impact line out without repeating it. Name the part the sources show was theirs, not the whole initiative others worked on too; a judgement of their part, such as "took ownership", says whose it is.
- Say only the impact the sources state, such as the points; a page's goal or an issue's purpose gives what the work aimed for, reached when the epic is Done or a source says so, unless another source shows it wasn't; a ticket closed without being done isn't finished work. Never infer it, and never measure it with counts of issues or pull requests: they show where the work went, not what it was worth. Every initiative has an impact line: when its impact isn't plain, look for it in the sources, as above, before you write.
- Read every point about an initiative's work before you write it, those of what to improve as much as those of what went well: the line and the paragraph say how the work went as the points put it, such as delays, few updates or unclear outcomes, beside what it achieved, each with whose view it is. State what the points say, not a verdict.
- Be conservative: claim no more than what you read shows. A title says only what an item is, so a claim that rests on a title alone says no more than that the person wrote it or did it; to say more, open it.
- Claim a share of the work, such as "most of" or "much of", only when a source states it, such as a feedback saying the person did most of an epic's stories: the brief holds the person's own items, not the whole, so a count can't show a share.
- Cite in `evidence` every point and item the paragraph rests on: a point by its id, such as G3, and an issue, epic, pull request or page by its link, as the brief gives it; a page you reached through an item is cited by that item. A group's search link isn't an item: cite the items of the group the paragraph rests on. The report shows them in a table under each initiative, so the line and the paragraph name no id and no link.
- Write about {{work_impact_line_words}} words for the impact line and {{work_impact_words}} for the paragraph, fewer when the evidence is thin: never pad.
